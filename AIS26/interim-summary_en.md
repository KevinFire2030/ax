## Professor Larry Heck's Session

The first major session was delivered by Professor Larry Heck of the Georgia Institute of Technology. The topic was "From AI Assistants to the Era of Agentic AI."

The central issue was the "autonomy trap": the expectation that an agentic AI system can fully understand a user's intent and complete a task from a single prompt alone. The speaker explained that real users do not present perfect requirements from the start, and that ambiguous requests and dynamic conversational flows make it difficult to properly evaluate agent performance using traditional single-turn benchmark methods.

He also cited several reasons why many enterprise AI agent pilots currently fail, including unclear user intent, lack of evaluation criteria, insufficient measurement of successful outcomes relative to cost, and difficulty with context switching.

## Historical Lessons

The speaker referred to his experience in speech recognition and deep learning research in the 1990s, pointing out that AI research and product development often repeat the mistake of not sufficiently learning from the past.

He mentioned the 1998 NIST evaluation, the large-scale deployment of speech deep learning in 1999, and later experiences with Cortana, Google Assistant, Bixby, and Viv Labs. His point was that today's agentic AI should revisit the lessons learned from earlier conversational and speech systems.

Key lessons included:

- Users do not always communicate clearly.
- Systems must be able to handle ambiguity.
- A process is needed to confirm whether the user's intent and the system's understanding are aligned.
- Text and speech alone are not enough; multimodal signals such as gaze, facial expression, and behavior should also be used.

## Evaluation And Cost

The speaker proposed "cost per successful outcome" as an important metric. In other words, dividing the total cost of an agent by the number of successful outcomes can serve as a practical measure of productivity and economic viability.

He noted that falling token prices do not automatically solve the overall cost problem. As agents perform more complex tasks over longer periods of time, costs may actually increase. Therefore, companies need to reduce costs while improving success rates through open-weight models, local agent loops, and infrastructure optimization.

## Multimodal AI And Human-AI Collaboration

During the Q&A, the discussion turned to how human-AI collaboration may change when agents gain the ability to see, hear, and sense.

Professor Larry Heck cited a 2014 experiment combining eye tracking and speech. The idea was that even when users do not express all information verbally, systems can better understand user intent by also observing gaze and behavioral signals.

The direction of multimodal models such as TwelveLabs was also mentioned. The point was that models capable of jointly understanding video, audio, images, text, language, and speech can strengthen the non-verbal intelligence of agents.

## Talent And Organizational Perspective

The speaker described the capabilities required for AI-native talent using elements he informally called the "4Cs." The main points can be summarized as curiosity, clear communication, creativity, and empathy.

It was especially emphasized that for AI to develop through interaction with human creativity, humans must open up diverse dimensions of data and experience. Human empathy is also important if AI is to learn empathy.

## Key Notes So Far

- The core challenge of agentic AI is the ability to infer and refine user intent even when users do not explain it perfectly.
- Single-turn evaluation and static benchmarks alone are insufficient for assessing real-world agent performance.
- Cost per successful outcome is likely to become an important operational metric.
- Multimodal signals are a key way to improve user intent understanding and human-AI collaboration.
- Lessons from past speech recognition and conversational AI systems should be applied again to current agentic AI development.
